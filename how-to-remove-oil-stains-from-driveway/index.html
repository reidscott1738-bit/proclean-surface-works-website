How to Remove Oil Stains From a Concrete Driveway

Why oil stains are so stubborn

Concrete looks solid, but it's actually porous, more like a hard sponge than a sealed slab. When motor oil drips onto it, the oil soaks down into those pores instead of sitting on top. That's why a quick rinse, or even a normal pressure wash, often just lightens an oil stain rather than removing it: the water blasts the surface, but the oil that's soaked below stays put. To really remove it, you have to pull the oil back up out of the concrete, not just clean the top.

Act fast on fresh spills

The easiest oil stain to remove is the one you catch early. If you see a fresh spill, cover it immediately with an absorbent like cat litter, cornstarch, or baking soda. Press it in, leave it for several hours or overnight to wick up the oil that hasn't soaked in yet, then sweep it up and dispose of it. The less oil that penetrates, the less you'll have to draw back out later.

Step-by-step: removing a set-in stain yourself

1. Absorb what you can. Even on an older stain, a fresh layer of absorbent left on overnight can pull up surface oil.

2. Degrease and scrub. Apply a concrete degreaser or a strong grease-cutting dish soap, let it dwell for 10 to 20 minutes without drying out, then scrub hard with a stiff nylon brush.

3. Rinse with the hottest water you can. Heat helps break down and lift oil. A hose helps; a pressure washer helps more. Repeat the degrease-and-scrub cycle for stubborn spots.

4. Use a poultice for deep stains. For old, set-in oil, make a poultice: mix a degreaser or acetone into an absorbent powder to form a paste, spread it thick over the stain, cover it with plastic, and let it dry for a day or two. As it dries, it draws oil back out of the pores. Scrape off and rinse.

Rust, transmission fluid, and other stains

Not every dark spot is motor oil. Rust stains (from patio furniture, rebar, or fertilizer) need a different, acidic rust remover, not a degreaser. Transmission and hydraulic fluid respond to the same degreasing approach as oil but can be more stubborn. If you're not sure what you're dealing with, that's a good reason to have a pro look, using the wrong product can set a stain rather than lift it.

When to call a professional

Old, deep, or large oil stains usually need commercial-grade degreasers, hot water, and proper pressure equipment to get real results, and even then, the honest truth is that very old stains sometimes only improve rather than vanish completely. A good pro will tell you that up front instead of promising a miracle.

Preventing the next stain

Once your driveway is clean, sealing the concrete is the best prevention. A quality concrete sealer fills the pores so future spills sit on the surface where they're easy to wipe up before they soak in. Reseal every few years. And keep a bag of cat litter in the garage, catching a spill in the first hour is still the single most effective 'stain remover' there is.

Questions

Straight answers.

Can you completely remove old oil stains?+

Often we can remove or greatly reduce them with commercial degreasers and hot pressure washing. Very old, deep stains sometimes only improve, and we'll tell you honestly what to expect before we start.

Will pressure washing alone remove oil?+

Usually not on its own. Oil soaks into porous concrete, so it needs a degreaser pretreatment plus hot water and pressure to draw it back out, not just a surface rinse.

Does sealing the driveway prevent stains?+

Yes. A concrete sealer fills the pores so future spills sit on top where they're easy to clean up before they soak in. It's the best long-term prevention.

We clean the whole driveway to an even finish so you're not left with one clean spot on a dirty slab.
